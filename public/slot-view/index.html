This week's bag is the washed Ethiopian from Kettle & Bean, currently and worth every penny. That price is not a screenshot and not my number: it is their element, updating as their page updates, laid out in my sentence like a word.

The card beside this paragraph is theirs too. I can float it, wrap text round its round edge, give it a shadow that follows its outline, and ask it to use my accent colour. What I can't do is read it, press its buttons behind your back, or see it at all before you tell the shop that's fine.

Brew it at 1:16, 94°C, three pours. Add a couple of bags with the card, watch the basket in the header follow, then press Buy now: the shop takes that one back and asks you itself.

    Takeaways
    
  
      
    
    • The box was the iframe's, not the content's. Once the provider sends pictures, the embed has the size, baseline and outline of the thing itself. It can be inline text, a float with shape-outside, or anything an image can be.
      • 
    
    • Consent has to happen where the embedder can't reach. Anything drawn from forwarded pixels and answered by forwarded clicks could be faked by the embedding page. So the question is asked in the provider's real frame, and its Allow button only works while the browser vouches the frame is uncovered and unfaded (IntersectionObserver v2). Small or inline embeds use a window on the provider's origin instead.
      • 
    
    • View and input are different things to agree to. A public price needs neither prompt nor input. A product card needs both. A purchase needs a real click every time, whatever was agreed.
      • 
    
    • The embedder can only say no. It can dismiss a question, give up its permission and ask again. It cannot answer yes.
      • 
    
    • Only the providing page needs HTML-in-canvas. The embedding page uses a canvas and a hidden frame. Where the provider lacks the API, the same element shows the frame as it is.
